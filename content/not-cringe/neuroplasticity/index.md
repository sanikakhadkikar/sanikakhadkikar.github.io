For a long time I believed humans can function just fine without emoting. Somewhere along the way it became hard for me to do, so I decided it wasn't important. Then someone who meant the world to me asked me for exactly that, and I realised that if I want to have a fulfilling personal life, I need to learn how to emote. As a scientist, I will always favour research-backed science to help me improve. If I could understand how emotions actually work in the brain and the body, maybe I could use that to show up better for myself and for the people I love. That's how I found neuroplasticity.

## What is neuroplasticity?

Neuroplasticity is the brain's ability to change itself. Every time you learn something, the connections between your neurons physically change, whether you are remembering the names of your new coworkers, getting better at a video game or learning a new route home.

Neuroscientists like to sum up how it works with the phrase "neurons that fire together, wire together". When two connected neurons keep firing at the same time, the connection between them gets stronger. The receiving neuron adds more receptors, so the same signal has a bigger effect the next time, and the pathway becomes the one your brain is most likely to use. This is called long-term potentiation. It also works the other way round, and connections that are rarely used together get weaker, which is called long-term depression (which has nothing to do with the mood).

{{< inline-svg src="synapse.svg" caption="Synaptic plasticity. A connection that is used a lot gains receptors and passes on a stronger signal, while one that is rarely used loses them." >}}

That is the synaptic side of things. There is also a structural side, where the neurons literally change shape. They grow tiny new bumps on their branches called dendritic spines, which is where new connections form, and they can branch out more. Connections that are not used get removed altogether in a process called synaptic pruning.

## Can adults still change?

Children's brains are plastic almost by default. They go through sensitive periods where they learn just by being around something, which is why a kid can pick up a language from playing with friends while I am on day 400 of Duolingo. A lot of what we learn passively as children stays with us for life, the good and the bad.

Adults can still change, but we have to work harder for it. Adult plasticity needs alertness and focused attention to switch on, small and incremental steps rather than giant leaps, and a lot of repetition. Sleep matters too, because that is when a lot of the strengthening actually gets locked in. It is harder at 26 than it was at 6, but it is very much possible.

## What does this have to do with emotions?

It turns out a lot. Emotional regulation runs on the same kind of wiring as everything else, and one of the key connections is between the amygdala, a small region that reacts quickly to threats and strong feelings, and the prefrontal cortex, the part behind your forehead that helps calm those reactions down. That connection changes with experience too. In [studies on rats](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4677120/), chronic stress made neurons in the amygdala grow more branches while neurons in the prefrontal cortex shrank theirs, so the alarm got louder and the brakes got weaker. Three weeks after the stress ended, the prefrontal neurons had grown back, although the amygdala changes lasted longer. A [2026 study in Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11203-1) is a reminder that this two-region story is a simplification, since there is no single "regulation centre" in the brain, but the direction of the evidence is the same.

{{< inline-svg src="stress-brain.svg" caption="A schematic of what chronic stress did to the rat brains, not to scale. Based on Vyas et al. (2002) and Radley et al. (2005)." >}}

What surprised me most was the research on holding emotions in. Psychologist James Gross and his colleagues found that when people [suppress the outward expression of their emotions](https://onlineacademiccommunity.uvic.ca/implicitassociationtestsyessir/wp-content/uploads/sites/9812/2025/12/gross-john-2003.pdf), the feeling does not go away and their bodies show more stress, even though they look calm on the outside. People who habitually suppress also feel less close to others, and [in one study](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-social-consequences-of-expressive-suppression.-Butler-Egloff/4b7cf3b63919bed5a48f8e9829ca942652e35c1c), even the person they were talking to had a rise in blood pressure. So not emoting does not just affect me, it affects the people I love too, and the chest ache and foggy thoughts I have been feeling suddenly made a lot more sense.

## Practices that actually rewire

If neurons that fire together wire together, then what I need are practices that I can repeat until a new pathway forms. Plenty of things make you feel better in the moment, but I wanted the ones where repeating them for weeks measurably changed the brain. There are fewer than I expected, but they exist.

The strongest is a [2026 study from the University of Illinois](https://news.illinois.edu/emotion-regulation-training-reduces-distress-changes-the-brain-team-reports/), published in Scientific Reports. College students trained twice a week for five weeks in two skills. The first was steering their attention towards neutral details instead of the upsetting centre of a scene, like looking at the trees and the sky in a photo of a car accident. The second was reappraisal, looking at a difficult situation from a different angle. Afterwards they reported less distress, even when thinking about hard events from their own lives, and their brain scans had changed, with the prefrontal cortex more connected and the regions linked to negative emotions less sensitive.

The second is resonance breathing, slowing your breath down to around six breaths a minute while an app or a sensor shows you your heart rate. In a [randomized trial](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13415-022-01032-w), young adults did this daily for five weeks, and the connection between the left amygdala and the medial prefrontal cortex got stronger. To be fair, the effect the researchers were mainly looking for on the right side of the brain did not show up, so this is promising rather than proven. A [review of studies from 2020 to 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13443423/) found it also leads to small to moderate drops in stress, anxiety and depression.

The third is distancing, practising looking at a painful situation as if from the outside, like a fly on the wall. In a [small 2024 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11305902/), five sessions over two weeks reduced the brain's negative emotion patterns, although this was in people with borderline personality disorder and the authors call it a proof of concept.

Something that surprised me is what did not make the list. Mindfulness meditation is often sold as rewiring the amygdala, but [two larger randomized trials](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.abk3316) failed to find the structural brain changes an [earlier small study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19776221/) reported. It can still help with anxiety and stress, but the rewiring claim is shaky.

And sleep, while not a practice in itself, is where a lot of this strengthening gets locked in. After one night without sleep, the amygdala was [over 60% more reactive](https://walkerlab.berkeley.edu/reprints/Yoo-Walker_CurrBiol_2007.pdf) and its link to the prefrontal cortex weakened, so skipping sleep undoes the very connection I am trying to build.

Neuroplasticity gives me the courage and the strength to keep going. It is hard, but no matter who you are, if you want to live a secure, happy and content life, the science says we can get there. I am 26, and I have time to do it.
