What do you like about your partner? Your future one if you are exploring right now, your current one if you are already in a relationship, or your past one if you're wallowing or grieving. Works either way. I asked this question to different age groups of people, take it as a social experiment if you may. The oldest dataset was my grandmother and my mentor, then I asked my parents and my best friend's mom, and then I asked my friends and coworkers.

The oldest test group measured their liking in years of companionship. It had its ups and downs, but they'll always be grateful for their partner. Interesting. My mom and dad talked about what each of them did for the other's parents, their children (the one and only me), and the community around them. Maybe that's the Indian parents in my dataset talking. My best friend's mom described her husband as, on average, a good father who takes care of and provides for their family. Interesting still.

The most interesting was my friends and coworkers, plus a biased data point, myself. Three of my coworkers, a varied dataset (one just got out of a relationship, one is in a long-term one, and one has been trying dating for a while), all had a, hmm, 'type'. 'They should know niche music, that's how I connect', 'They like anime, otherwise we would be bored together', 'We go out together so much, it's a fun time', 'I will never date someone who likes exercising that much'. See it? Interesting.

A simple question got such different answers. First came the person, then what the person did for everyone around them, then the silhouettification of the person into a bunch of qualities. I'm part of that last group and it shocked me a little to hear it from the outside. It's dehumanising in a sense, to look for someone who fits your checklist when what you want is someone who makes you content, makes you happy. In my head, I think of this as an over-optimisation of our social lives, and it is scary because my hands are red too.

So where did my generation's checklist come from?

## Waiting for "the one"

I think part of it is that there's some truth to the "Girl, stop crying over him, you deserve better" we keep hearing. Sometimes you do deserve better. But we keep saying it to ourselves without even realising it, until we're back to looking for the perfect "one".

Psychologists have a name for this. In 1998, Raymond Knee described two ways people think about relationships. **Destiny** is the belief that a relationship is either meant to be or not. **Growth** is the belief that it's something you build over time. When things got hard, people who believed more in **destiny** were [more likely to disengage](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.74.2.360). You stop bringing up what's bothering you, you stop trying to resolve the fight, and you start thinking the whole thing was doomed anyway. If you believe in destiny, the checklist makes sense. You are not building something with someone, you are searching for the one person who already ticks every box.

## Does the one even exist?

The physicist in me wanted to know whether that person even exists. Statistically, sure. Out of eight billion people, there is probably someone out there who ticks every box. Finding them is a different story. Randall Munroe of xkcd once worked out what would happen if everyone had exactly one soulmate, born somewhere random in the world. Most of them would be long dead or not born yet, and only about [one in 10,000 people](https://what-if.xkcd.com/9/) would ever meet theirs. That is about the same as your lifetime odds of being [struck by lightning](https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-odds), roughly 1 in 15,000. It can happen, but most people I know haven't been. Waiting for the one is a bit like waiting to be struck by lightning.

## The art of staying

If the perfect match is that unlikely, then the person in front of you was never going to tick every box, and that is fine. Some of their qualities will be different from yours, like a difference in humour, a difference in preferred pastimes or a difference in opinion on *Brooklyn Nine-Nine* (my favourite show, haha).

What matters is what you do with those differences, and Robin Williams says it best in *Good Will Hunting*. As the therapist Sean, he tells Will about his late wife, who used to fart in her sleep, and how those little quirks are what he misses the most. ["People call those imperfections, but no, that's the good stuff."](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0119217/quotes) Williams improvised most of that story, and Matt Damon's laughter in the scene is [completely real](https://uk.news.yahoo.com/robin-williams-improvised-line-good-072740685.html). The differences never stopped Sean loving his wife. They became part of why he did.

Which brings me back to my grandmother's answer. She did not list a single quality. She talked about years of companionship. So instead of the checklist, I would ask different questions. Do you smile with them? Do you laugh with them? Have you genuinely never felt safer with anyone else? That is what I think should matter.

The research agrees with her. A study that pooled data from [43 studies covering 11,196 couples](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/8mc5n2c3) found that the best predictors of a happy relationship were things like how appreciated you feel and how committed you think your partner is, not either person's traits. And John Gottman found that about [69% of the conflicts couples have](https://www.gottman.com/blog/managing-conflict-solvable-vs-perpetual-problems/) never get solved, happy couples included. The differences don't go away. People just stay.

## Learning to stay in these modern times

This is where I can get personal, because my checklist was never just for partners. I used it on friends too. All my friends should do X, my partner should do Y with me, and if they don't do Z, am I even compatible with them? I can always find new friends who accept me and have the same tastes. I used to think that, and in response, one of my wise friends told me this in one line. 'The cost of community, of whatever kind, is discomfort.'

Learning to sit with that discomfort is what emotional regulation has meant for me. When I couldn't, the discomfort always won. I would shut down instead of saying how I truly felt, and I would stop listening.

And listening is where the differences show up the most. In lives this optimised to succeed, grow, earn and somehow also be physically okay, it feels like there is no room for 'useless' things. At least it did for me. My friend telling me about the seven kinds of mithai he had in Banaras, another telling me about his RuneScape rally, my mother telling me what that relative said to her. None of it would pass my old checklist. But all of it means something to them, and if they mean something to me, it should mean something to me too. That's what [emotional regulation](/caricature/neuroplasticity/) looks like in practice. Being there and listening, no matter how frivolous the thing is, no matter how uninterested I personally am in it, or how much I'd rather be doing anything else. It isn't about me. They share because it's love, no matter what they share.

The last couple of months have been some of the hardest of my life, and they taught me the importance of love, of community and of being realistic about relationships. I suddenly had a lot of time to hyper-optimise my life, to pursue whatever I wanted and to work as hard as, or even harder than, ever before, ignoring my loved ones as usual. Instead, and rather fortunately, I slowed down, and today I am supported better than ever, even if I am not doing great on the career front. When I look back on the last couple of months, I don't remember my career or my accolades. I remember my family and friends who stayed, and none of them stayed because I ticked their boxes. They taught me all this.
