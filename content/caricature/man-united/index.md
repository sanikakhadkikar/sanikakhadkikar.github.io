Manchester United, one of the most famous football clubs in the world, is listed on the New York Stock Exchange, right next to companies like Coca-Cola and Nike. Its ticker symbol is MANU. If you have a trading app on your phone, you can buy a share of Manchester United in about thirty seconds. I wanted to know why they listed in New York and whether the owners of any club could do the same.

## A club bought with its own money

The story starts in 2005, when the Glazers, an American family who also own the Tampa Bay Buccaneers in American football, bought Manchester United. They paid for most of it with borrowed money, and then moved that debt onto the club itself. In other words, United was bought using loans that United then had to pay back. This kind of deal is called a leveraged buyout, and it is common in business, but it was new to English football.

Fans were furious. Some were so angry that they left and started an entirely new club, FC United of Manchester, which still plays in the lower leagues today. The debt did not go away either. Since 2005, United has paid more than [£1 billion in interest](https://www.insideworldfootball.com/2025/12/12/man-utds-first-quarter-financials-reports-debt-spiralling-past-1bn/) alone, money that went to banks instead of players, coaches or the stadium.

## Going public, in New York

In 2012, the Glazers sold part of the club on the stock market, which is called an initial public offering, or IPO. They first tried in Singapore and pulled out after weak interest, then looked at London, and finally [chose New York](https://the-cfo.io/2012/09/20/getting-on-the-public-list/). That is an odd choice for a football club in a country where "football" means a different sport entirely.

The likely reason is in the fine print. United created two kinds of shares. The [Class A shares](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1549107/000104746912008161/a2210672z424b4.htm) sold to the public came with one vote each, while the Class B shares the Glazers kept came with ten votes each. That kind of two-tier setup was acceptable in New York but [much harder to do in London](https://wan-saifullah.blogspot.com/2012/07/the-manchester-united-ipo-some-initial_5.html). So the public could buy a piece of the club, but the family kept control of it.

Even the launch did not go to plan. The shares were [sold at \$14](https://www.renaissancecapital.com/IPO-Center/News/12246/Manchester-United-prices-IPO-at-%2414-below-the-range), below the $16 to $20 the club had hoped for, raising about $233 million. Half of that went towards paying down the debt, and the other half went straight to the Glazers.

Today, the Glazers still own the majority of the club. In 2024, the British billionaire Jim Ratcliffe and his company INEOS bought a [27.7% stake for about £1.3 billion](https://www.africanews.com/2024/03/12/man-united-ratcliffe-has-already-cost-nearly-44-billion//) and took over running the football side. If you buy a share today, you will technically own part of Manchester United, but your vote will be worth one tenth of a Glazer's, and there are far fewer of yours.

## Tottenham listed first

Football clubs on stock exchanges are not new. Tottenham Hotspur became the [first football club in the world to float on a stock exchange](https://www.tottenhamhotspur.com/the-club/history/year-by-year/) back in 1983, in London. In the 1990s, many English clubs followed, hoping to raise money as football became big business. Most of them, including Tottenham, eventually left the stock market again, and it's easy to see why. Clubs spend most of what they earn on wages and transfers, and their value depends on whether a ball goes into a net.

## Who can buy a club?

In England, more or less anyone. If you have enough money and a club's owners are willing to sell, the club can be bought and loaded with debt, as United was. Some countries have decided they do not want that.

In Germany, the [50+1 rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/50%2B1_rule) says a club's members must always hold at least 50% of the votes plus one. Investors can put money in, but they cannot take control. A few exceptions exist for clubs that a company has funded for more than twenty years, like Bayer Leverkusen, which grew out of the Bayer pharmaceutical company, and Wolfsburg, which is tied to Volkswagen. A Glazer-style takeover of a club like Borussia Dortmund is barred under that rule.

Spain went in a different direction. In 1990, a new sports law forced Spanish professional clubs to turn into companies with shareholders. Only four clubs that had healthy finances at the time were [allowed to stay as member-owned associations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociedad_An%C3%B3nima_Deportiva). They were Real Madrid, Barcelona, Athletic Bilbao and Osasuna. These clubs belong to their members, the *socios*, who elect the club president, so nobody can buy them, not the Glazers, not a billionaire, not a country.

That does not mean member ownership is perfect. Members can vote for presidents who make terrible decisions too. The difference is who gets a say afterwards. At a member-owned club, the people who live with the mistakes get to vote on what happens next. At United, fans can buy a share in their club, but they cannot buy a say in it.
