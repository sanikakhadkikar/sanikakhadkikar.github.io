## Where the idea came from

In 1950, communist North Korea invaded South Korea, and the United States, deep into the Cold War and determined to stop communism spreading, led a United Nations force to push it back. China joined on the North Korean side, and thousands of American soldiers became prisoners of war. A few of them later read out confessions on Chinese radio saying the US had used germ warfare, and a small number refused to come home when the war ended. Americans called it [brainwashing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brainwashing) and became convinced the communists had a secret technique to wipe out a person's beliefs and replace them with new ones. If mind control was possible, Western governments wanted to understand how it worked and how to defend against it.

That worry led to [MKUltra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MKUltra), a research program approved in April 1953. The name was a code, not a description. "MK" was a [prefix used](https://www.maryferrell.org/php/cryptdb.php?id=MK) for projects run by a scientific and technical division, and "Ultra" may have been a nod to the Second World War, when Ultra was the British code word for intelligence from broken Enigma messages. Over the next twenty years it funded more than 150 projects at more than 80 universities, hospitals, prisons and pharmaceutical companies across the United States and Canada, at an estimated cost of $10 million or more. The research looked at LSD, sensory deprivation, hypnosis and electric shock treatment, and according to the records that later came out, some of it was done on people who had not given consent. Standards for medical consent were very different in the 1950s, and much of what is now required in research grew out of cases like these.

## Testing outside the lab

Some of the testing happened outside labs and hospitals. The records describe an operation called Midnight Climax, in which apartments in San Francisco were set up to observe how people behaved after being given LSD without knowing it. One of the best-known cases from this period is that of [Frank Olson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Olson), an army scientist who was given LSD without his knowledge at a work retreat in 1953 and died after falling from a New York hotel window about a week later.

MKUltra funded LSD studies that paid volunteers to take the drug, and one of those volunteers was a Stanford student named Ken Kesey. A few years later, Kesey and his friends started throwing huge LSD parties in California, with live music and light shows, that they called the [Acid Tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_Tests). The name was theirs, but the parties helped spread LSD into the counterculture of the 1960s. A drug studied as a possible tool for mind control ended up becoming one of the symbols of the 1960s.

## Depatterning in Montreal

{{< photo float="right" src="mkultra-doc.jpg" alt="A typed page from a declassified document with sections blacked out" caption="A page from the declassified MKUltra documents, released under a Freedom of Information Act request." source="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:DeclassifiedMKULTRA.jpg" license="public domain" >}}

The darkest part of the story happened at the Allan Memorial Institute in Montreal, where the program funded the psychiatrist [Ewen Cameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Ewen_Cameron) from 1957 to 1964. Cameron believed in what he called depatterning, wiping out a person's mind until they were reduced to an infantile state, and then building a new personality on top. His patients had mostly come in for depression or anxiety, and were never told they were part of an experiment.

He kept them in drug-induced sleep for weeks, waking them [two or three times a day for electroshock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreal_experiments) at doses some accounts put at [twenty to forty times](https://spartacus-educational.com/JFKcameronDE.htm) the normal level. Then came psychic driving, recorded messages played to them for up to 16 hours a day, first negative ones about their own lives and failings, then positive ones meant to install the new self. Many left unable to remember their families, their past lives or even basic everyday skills, and many never recovered.

## How it all came out

In 1973, most of the MKUltra files were destroyed. About 20,000 pages survived because they had been filed with financial records, and [John Marks](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/node/4567), a former State Department official, dug them out through Freedom of Information Act requests. They led to US Senate hearings in 1977, where the program was examined in public, and to his 1979 book *The Search for the "Manchurian Candidate"*.

Some compensation followed. Olson's family received a settlement and an apology from President Ford in 1975. Nine of Cameron's patients reached a settlement with the US government in 1988, and Canada paid 77 former patients in 1992. The Montreal patients have never received a formal apology, and only in July 2025 did a Quebec court allow a [class action](https://www.ctvnews.ca/montreal/article/mk-ultra-mind-control-class-action-authorized-by-quebec-superior-court/) by them and their families to go ahead.

## What the prisoners reported

If brainwashing was not real, why did the prisoners in Korea act the way they did? The psychologist [Edgar Schein](https://www.militarypsych.org/wp-content/uploads/05-Hamilton-Spotlight-Edgar-Schein.pdf) interviewed them as they came home in 1953 and later called what they went through coercive persuasion. Their captors kept them isolated, cold, hungry and exhausted, interrogated them over and over, and asked for small things first, like a signature or a short statement, that slowly turned into bigger ones. Most of the prisoners cooperated at some point in ways that seemed trivial to them at the time, and almost none of them came out with different beliefs. In 1956, the US Army concluded it could not find [even one conclusively documented case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brainwashing) of an American prisoner being brainwashed.

The US military drew its own lesson from this. In 1955, President Eisenhower signed a [Code of Conduct](https://www.kunsan.af.mil/News/Article/414344/the-military-code-of-conduct-a-brief-history/) for American soldiers in captivity, and the military created survival and resistance training, known as SERE, that put soldiers through these same pressures so they would recognise them if they were ever captured. The techniques were studied in detail by the sociologist Albert Biderman, whose [chart of coercive methods](https://traces.brynmawr.edu/exhibits/show/brainwashing/intro/serebiderman.html) was taught in that training for decades.

After twenty years, MKUltra had documented ways to frighten people, disorient them and damage their memory. It had failed to demonstrate mind control.
