In 2016, I lost someone very close to me. I was young, and I don't think I really processed it. Then, in 2021, COVID took my grandmother, and that was when I properly fell apart. By then I was studying physics, and I found myself wanting to know two things. Could physics prove or disprove the existence of ghosts, and what do people even mean by a ghost? I kept seeing my grandma in things, or feeling like she was the one doing them. She had never once appeared in my dreams, until the night before she passed away, when she came and told me to get home safe.

It is Friday the 13th today, so I thought I would finally try to answer those questions properly.

## What do we mean by a ghost?

Most people, when they say ghost, mean something of a person that survives their death and can still interact with our world. It can be seen, heard, felt in a room, or it moves things. A lot of people believe they have met one. In a [Pew Research Center survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2015/10/30/18-of-americans-say-theyve-seen-a-ghost/), 18% of American adults said they had seen a ghost or been in its presence, and 29% said they had felt in touch with someone who had died.

## People who tried to measure ghosts

The first person to take this question seriously as a physics problem was a doctor in Haverhill, Massachusetts, named Duncan MacDougall. His idea was simple. If a soul is real and it leaves the body at death, then maybe it has mass, and if it has mass, a good enough scale should catch the body getting lighter. Starting in 1901, he put the beds of six dying patients on a large, very sensitive scale and [watched the numbers as they died](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/21_grams_experiment). One patient lost about three quarters of an ounce, around 21 grams, at the moment of death. That one result is where the idea that the soul weighs 21 grams comes from.

The rest of the data is the part nobody remembers. Of the other patients, one lost weight and then gained it back, two lost more weight a few minutes after dying, and two results he threw out because the scale was not adjusted properly or the patient died while he was still setting it up. He also weighed fifteen dogs as they died and found no change at all, which he took as evidence that dogs have no souls. A critic at the time pointed out a far more boring explanation. As the body stops regulating itself, it can briefly heat up and sweat, and dogs barely sweat. With six patients, one supporting result and a lot of discarded data, it would not pass as a physics experiment today, and it has never been repeated.

Then there was Thomas Edison. In October 1920, he told [*The American Magazine*](https://www.mentalfloss.com/history/thomas-edison-nikola-tesla-spirit-phone) that he was working on an apparatus sensitive enough to detect whether the personalities of dead people could still communicate with us. Readers sent in hundreds of letters. The machine, though, was never shown to anyone, and no blueprints or prototypes have ever been found. Historians still argue over whether he was serious or just playing with reporters.

## The floor problem

In films, ghosts walk through walls. But they also walk across floors, climb stairs and sit on chairs. To walk, you have to push against the floor, and the floor has to push back, which is Newton's third law. Something that passes straight through a wall cannot also push against a floor made of exactly the same kind of atoms. A ghost has to pick one.

Seeing a ghost runs into the same problem. To be seen, a ghost has to give off light or bounce it into your eyes, which means it interacts with electromagnetism. Electromagnetism is also the force that stops your hand from going through a table, because the electrons in your hand and the table push each other away. So anything solid enough to be seen should be solid enough to bump into walls. Physics cannot rule out something that never interacts with anything at all, but nobody would ever see or hear a ghost like that either.

In 1921, an eye doctor named William Wilmer published the story of a family he called Mr. and Mrs. H. In their new house they heard footsteps and bells at night, felt hands on them in bed and saw strange figures, while the whole family was getting headaches and feeling exhausted. A doctor eventually found that their old furnace was [leaking carbon monoxide](https://www.iflscience.com/health-and-medicine/100yearold-haunted-house-mystery-solved-by-professor-and-ophthalmologist/) into the house. Carbon monoxide is a gas with no colour and no smell, made when fuel does not burn properly. Once you breathe it in, it grabs onto haemoglobin, the molecule in your blood that carries oxygen, [over 200 times more strongly](https://wikem.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide_toxicity) than oxygen does. So the more of it you breathe, the less oxygen reaches your body, and the brain, which uses more oxygen than almost any other organ, suffers first. At low levels it causes [headaches, nausea, dizziness and tiredness](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK153687/), exactly what the family felt, and as it builds up, it brings confusion and agitation. A brain running short of oxygen is much worse at making sense of what it sees and hears, which makes it very easy to read a creak as a footstep or a shadow as a figure. The family moved out until the furnace was repaired, and when they came back, the haunting was gone.

## Grief is the most common ghost

That still left me wondering about my grandmother. In 1971, a Welsh doctor named Dewi Rees interviewed 293 widowed people in his practice and found that [nearly half](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1799198/) had seen, heard or felt their dead husband or wife. The most common form was a sense that the person was still there. Most of them had never told anyone, not even their doctor, and Rees concluded that these experiences were a normal and often helpful part of grieving.

The most moving version of this I found comes from Japan. After the earthquake and tsunami of March 2011 killed thousands of people in the north-east of the country, a sociology student named Yuka Kudo made weekly trips to Ishinomaki, one of the worst-hit cities, to [interview taxi drivers](https://www.thedrive.com/news/2053/after-japans-tsunami-taxi-drivers-carry-ghosts-trying-to-get-home). Seven of them told her about passengers who vanished. One driver picked up a woman near the station who asked to go to a neighbourhood the tsunami had flattened. When he told her there was almost nothing left there, she asked, "Have I died?" When he looked back, the seat was empty. Some of the drivers had started their meters, and showed Kudo the records of fares that were never paid. What struck Kudo most was that none of the drivers were afraid. They spoke about these passengers with reverence, and she wondered whether the taxis had become a private room for a grieving city.

A [2021 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8388006/) of nearly 1,000 people found the same thing, with one detail that stopped me. The most common way people experienced someone who had died was during sleep, in a dream. Nearly three quarters said the experience brought them comfort and emotional healing. The researchers argued that doctors who brush these experiences off as a symptom can do real harm, because for most people they are a way of staying connected to someone they loved.

So when I kept seeing my grandma in things, nothing was wrong with me. My brain had spent my whole life learning her voice, her habits and the way she moved through a room, and it did not just switch that off when she was gone.

## Get home safe

I don't know of anything in physics that could carry a person's mind after death and still let them knock on a door or appear in a hallway.

What physics cannot tell me is what that dream meant. She had never come in my dreams before, and she came the night before she died to tell me to get home safe. I know all the reasonable explanations. I still would not trade that dream for any of them.
